Hamlet can't make up his mind. That's the whole engine of the play. His dad's dead, murdered by Claudius, and the ghost wants revenge. Pretty simple job, right? Not for Hamlet.

The soliloquies show us why. "To be or not to be" isn't just a speech — it's a guy stuck in his own head, weighing what happens after you die against the misery of staying alive. He thinks too much. Honestly, that's his problem from act one onward.

He wants to do the right thing. He also wants to be sure first. Those two urges don't get along, and the gap between them drags the story forward — scene after scene of him circling, stalling, watching.

But Shakespeare isn't only writing a revenge tragedy here. He's poking at bigger stuff. What are we? Why are we here? What comes after? Hamlet's hesitation turns into a doorway for those questions.

Critics point out something else too. The Renaissance was a time when old medieval certainties were getting shaken by new ways of asking questions, and Hamlet's wobbling fits that moment exactly.

So why do we still watch it? Because we recognize him. Contemplation versus action. Thinking versus doing.
